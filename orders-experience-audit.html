Orders experience audit

The customer Orders tab (/orders) and the order tracking screen (/orders/[id]), read as a customer rather than as a diff. 13 September 2026 — second pass, findings 10–13 closed.

How this was read

Both screens were walked through as a person with a phone in their hand and a specific question, not as a component tree. Every finding below starts from a sentence a customer would actually say. Where the fix was free — the data was already fetched and on the row, unused — it is marked as shipped in this pass.

Scenarios

“Where is my food, is it close?”

The live order was the same row as the seven finished ones under it, separated by an orange chevron. Its one line of 13px grey text said when the order was placed — “Tue, 10:39 pm · Preparing” — which is the one fact nobody opens this tab to learn. The arrival time existed, computed by lib/orders/eta.ts, one tap away.

“Which one of these was the biryani?”

Eight rows reading Saffron Kitchen · ₹302 · Delivered. The items were fetched with every row (order_items(name, qty, price…) is in the list query) and shown nowhere. For a customer with one regular restaurant, the history was an undifferentiated column — and “order again” was a coin flip.

“The one from last month, with the naans.”

formatOrderPlacedAt dropped the date entirely past yesterday: every older order rendered as a weekday and a time, so last Wednesday and Wednesday five months ago were the same four characters. There was no search, no filter and no grouping over a list capped at 100 rows.

“Half of these never even arrived.”

Five of eight rows in the reference screenshot were cancellations, visually identical to deliveries but for the colour of one word. Nothing let a customer ask for only the orders that actually happened.

“I paid online and it was cancelled — where’s my money?”

The list said “Cancelled” and stopped. payment_status was already on the row and knows the difference between money we returned and money we are still holding.

“What is this grey band under the buttons?”

On the tracking screen the sheet ended with its content and the shell’s reserved 80px tab-bar strip showed through beneath it, with the sheet’s own drop shadow smeared across the seam. The sheet also drew a grab handle for a gesture that did not exist: it was an ordinary block in a scrolling page.

What changed

The Orders tab

  • A live order card under a “Happening now” heading: restaurant, order code, total, the arrival estimate as the headline, a four-notch progress bar, the current stage in words, and a “running N minutes late” chip when the ETA says so. The estimate is the same OrderEta the tracking screen headlines, fetched server-side and refreshed on the existing 10s cadence, so the two screens can never disagree. An unpinned shop yields “On its way” rather than a kitchen’s advertised band dressed up as an arrival time.
  • History rows say what was in the order — “Paneer Butter Masala +2 more” — and carry a real date past the first week.
  • Filter chips with counts (All / Delivered / Cancelled), a search box over restaurant and dish names once there are six or more orders, and month headings so scrolling has landmarks. Filtering is client-side because the whole list is already on the page.
  • “Refund not issued” on a cancelled order whose payment is still paid. Worded as the ledger state, not as a promise — a request may already be in flight.
  • Reorder is labelled “Again”, and a filter that matches nothing gets its own notice with a way back, distinct from having no history at all.

The tracking screen

  • The sheet drags. Three stops — 8%, 34% and 66% of the area under the header. The handle drags with a finger, a mouse or the arrow keys and toggles on tap; a finger anywhere in the content also moves it, but only when the pull cannot mean anything else (the list is at its top and the finger is going down, or the sheet is not fully open and the finger is going up), so an ordinary scroll is never stolen.
  • No more grey band. The screen fills the frame and scrolls nothing at the page level; the sheet runs to the bottom edge with the tab bar floating over it, and the content clears the bar.
  • The map is sized to the sheet’s lowest stop, so dragging down runs out of travel exactly at the bottom of the map and can never expose the page behind it.
  • The map knows it is partly covered. The bounds fit, the pan that follows the courier, the “road route unavailable” captions and the no-SDK schematic all subtract the covered strip, and the zoom control moved out of the corner the sheet sits on. A finished order opens with the sheet up, since there is nothing left to watch.

Findings 10–13, in the second pass

Cancellations now say who and why (11)

Migration 0051 adds cancelled_by and cancellation_reason to orders, on two different footings. cancelled_by is evidence: it is on guard_order_update()’s locked list and derived by the lifecycle trigger from whoever is actually making the write, so a vendor cannot pre-write “customer” onto an order they are about to reject. cancellation_reason is a sentence, and is deliberately left writable — the kitchen rejecting an order is who should be typing it — but only on the update that performs the cancellation itself.

Above it, the kitchen board’s window.confirm (“Reject order X?”, answer discarded) becomes a dialog with four one-tap reasons written from the customer’s side of the conversation, plus free text. The reason is optional on purpose: a kitchen mid-rush must still be able to reject in one tap, and a required field would be answered with “.” inside a week. The admin console already collected a reason and filed it against the refund, where the customer never saw it; it now goes on the order too. Both customer screens render it, and render nothing when the row cannot be attributed — a pre-0051 cancellation — because the guess everyone reaches for is the accusation.

Receipts (10)

/orders/[id]/receipt: line items, subtotal, discount and coupon code, delivery, taxes, tip, total, how it was paid and where it went. Every one of those was already fetched with the order and thrown away during mapping. Delivered orders only — a receipt for a cancelled order is a document asserting a sale that did not happen, and it is the kind of document people forward to an accountant. Printing rather than a bundled PDF renderer, with the @media print block extended to unwind the phone frame: without that, printing gives one phone-shaped screenshot with the rest clipped off.

Paging (12)

GET /api/orders/history?before=<iso> pages past the 100-row cap, keyed on created_at rather than an offset so an order arriving mid-scroll cannot shift a page boundary and show somebody the same row twice. The cap is now visible — a “Load older orders” control instead of a wall that looked like the end of your history — and while a search is active the list says plainly that it is searching only what has been loaded.

The delivery code, pinned (follow-up)

It was a tinted card four blocks into a scrolling sheet — present, but something you had to go and find while a courier waited at the door. It now lives in the sheet’s header, above the scroller, so it is on screen at every stop including the one where the sheet is pushed down to show the map. A hairline and two words of label; the digits carry it.

Pull to refresh (13)

The timer was right to only run while an order was in flight; what was missing was any way to ask. Pulling did nothing, and not by omission: .app-scroll sets overscroll-behavior: contain, which suppresses even the browser’s own gesture. The new component drives that scroller directly so the page follows the finger, and AutoRefresh gained an interval={0} mode that keeps the catch-up-on-focus without polling a page of finished orders.
