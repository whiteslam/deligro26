Profile experience audit

The customer Account tab and every screen under it — saved addresses, notifications, help & support, privacy & security, favourites, about. 14 September 2026.

Scenarios

“Something happened to my order — let me check the bell.”

The bell in the header opened a settings screen: a permission switch and two rows of text describing categories nobody could change. Tapping a bell and getting preferences is the app answering a question you did not ask. And there was nothing else for it to open — push goes to OneSignal and nothing about it is stored: no table, no history, no read state. A customer who missed the “your rider is on the way” banner had no way to see it again, ever.

“What do they know about me?”

The account screen offers Privacy & security with a shield icon. It pointed at /profile/help — the same destination as the Help & support row directly above it. Two labels, two icons, one screen, and the screen that arrived was a delivery FAQ that mentions neither privacy nor security.

“I’ve moved. Delete the old address.”

If it was your default, you were left with no default at all — nothing promoted another. No error appeared, because every screen that wants a default is written as find(isDefault) ?? list[0]. So it failed quietly instead: the Default badge vanished, “Set default” appeared on every row, and checkout silently began preferring whichever address happened to sort first.

“Why won’t it let me remove this one?”

It would. The Remove button on a default address was rendered at 50% opacity — the universal sign for “you cannot do this” — and was never disabled, so it worked anyway. The worst of both: it discouraged a legitimate action without preventing it, and the thing it was discouraging was the bug above.

“Wait — what am I agreeing to?”

The sign-in screen states: “By continuing you agree to Deligro’s Terms & Conditions and Privacy Policy.” That is plain text. Not a link, because neither document exists anywhere in the app — there is nothing to read and nothing to point at.

“Is this the same app?”

Every profile screen uses ProfileSubpage — a 22px title beside a round back button, scrolling with the page. Saved addresses rolls its own: a 17px title in a sticky glass bar with a second action on the right. It is the one screen under Account that does not look like the others.

What changed

The bell now means what a bell means

/profile/notifications opens with what actually happened — order sent, kitchen started cooking, packed, on the way, delivered, cancelled — newest first, each entry linking back to the order it is about, and a cancellation carrying the reason the kitchen gave. Settings move below it, where settings belong.

No new table. The events a customer was pushed about are the same events the order lifecycle already timestamps — created_at, accepted_at, ready_at, cancelled_at (migration 0026) — so the feed is derived from facts the database already holds rather than from a log nobody is writing. That makes it honest by construction: nothing can appear that did not happen, and nothing goes missing because a push failed. The cost is no read state, and no entry for anything that was not a lifecycle change. Worth it until there is a reason to write rows.

Privacy & security is a real screen

It describes what the app actually holds, field by field — your phone number and why, addresses and when they are saved, order history and why it is kept rather than cleared, the push device token and what turning notifications off does to it — plus how sign-in works (a code, never a password) and what you can do about each. Every claim on it is checkable against the schema.

Addresses stop lying in two directions

deleteAddress now hands the default flag to the oldest remaining address when it removes the one carrying it — server-side, so every caller gets it, and best-effort, because the deletion is what the customer asked for and has already happened. With the underlying problem fixed, the Remove button drops its decorative dimming. Confirmation moved from window.confirm to the app’s own dialog, which can name the address and say that the default will move on.

One thing deliberately not done. Finding 6 is a privacy policy and terms of service. I did not write them. Inventing legal text to fill a screen would be worse than the broken row it replaced — the new Privacy & security page describes real behaviour and says nothing it cannot back up. When a real policy exists it belongs at the bottom of that page and behind the two words on the sign-in screen that currently link nowhere.

Not done, and worth deciding on

  • Editing an address (7). The API supports an update; the screen offers add and remove only. A customer correcting a house number has to delete and re-add, which until today also cost them their default.
  • Page chrome (8). Saved addresses could adopt ProfileSubpage with a right-hand action slot, so all six screens under Account share one header.
  • Per-category notification preferences (9). The page is honest that they do not exist. Making them real needs somewhere to store them and a check at send time.
